02  //  Selected project

Short-term rental pricing and demand pipeline

Hotel revenue management runs on three numbers: occupancy, average daily rate, and RevPAR. Short-term rental data is almost never analyzed that way, because the public data contains no bookings at all. It does contain reviews, and reviews are a documented proxy for stays.

Airflow ingests Inside Airbnb snapshots for Austin, dbt models them on a DuckDB warehouse, and the output is those three metrics by neighbourhood and month plus a backtested weekly demand forecast.

The forecast is seasonal naive with drift, scored across twelve rolling origins at a twelve week horizon: 7.83% MAPE, 34% better than the baseline, with prediction intervals taken from backtest residuals rather than assumed.

Forecasts are keyed on training vintage, so every past forecast stays scoreable against what actually happened. Read the code →

03  //  What it found

The pipeline was the easy half

The useful half was what it turned up in its own inputs, and which of my own ideas the measurements threw out.

01

A snapshot with every listing and no prices

The December 2025 scrape holds all 11,251 listings with the price column empty for every single one. A row-count check passes it without complaint. It had been anchoring the as-of rate lookup, which silently emptied the fact table. Completeness and rate-usability became separate flags, because a snapshot can be perfectly good for sizing a market and useless for pricing it.

02

A scrape missing a quarter of the market

March 2026 returned 8,170 listings against a median of 11,181. Tracing listing IDs across snapshots, 2,533 listings present in both December and June are absent from March, which is 84% of the gap. A listing cannot leave a market and come back, so those were missed rather than delisted. Both defects now trip a test on every build.

03

A trend that was an artifact, not growth

Reviews climb from 31 in 2009 to 146,569 in 2025, and almost none of that is the market growing: listings delisted before the first snapshot never appear at all. Every model with a free trend parameter lost to a trend-free baseline, and rerunning the backtest with an extra year of history made each fitted model measurably worse. That is a cleaner proof of the distortion than any argument about it.

04

A fix that worked in theory and failed in practice

Six weeks out of 56 carry 34% of all forecast error, and the biggest cluster is festival timing: SXSW peaked on 10 March in 2025 and 16 March in 2026, so a year-ago lookup misses twice. Blending the surrounding weeks fixes it on a synthetic series built to contain the effect, then loses on real data. That model stayed in the repository as the reason the simpler one shipped.
